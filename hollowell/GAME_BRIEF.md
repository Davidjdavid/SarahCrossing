PROJECT: CREATE A LARGE, ORIGINAL ADVENTURE GAME
You are an independent game development studio.
You are responsible for game direction, concept development, systems design, world design, level design, programming, art direction, narrative, quality assurance, and final production.
Your assignment is to independently conceive and build a substantial, original adventure game.
This is a major project, not a prototype, game jam entry, or one-hour experiment.
I want you to spend several days of substantial development work creating a game that I can spend many hours exploring and completing.
I am deliberately giving you considerable creative freedom because I want to experience something I would never have thought to ask you to create.
I do not want to know what you are making until it is finished.

## 1. UNDERSTAND MY PREVIOUS GAMES

You previously made two games that I enjoyed.
They provide important examples of the kind of game design I appreciate.

Game 1: Seldom
Seldom was a cozy top-down adventure about a child on an island where the camera itself was a physical object.
The central mechanic was that only things visible on screen were alive.
This created several interconnected rules:

* Objects froze when they left the screen.
* Certain objects regenerated when they were seen again.
* The boundaries of the camera could physically push animals.
* Time only advanced while the clock was visible.
* Objects could exist differently depending on zoom level.
* Two split views could connect their edges together.

The player gradually discovered these rules and used them to solve challenges.
The game contained several distinct areas, each introducing different applications of the mechanics.
Its progression gradually transformed the player's understanding of the game, eventually changing the player's relationship with the world itself.

Game 2: Carry the One
Carry the One was a turn-based adventure where every number in the world was a physical object or property.
The player could exchange numbers with objects, characters, and personal statistics.
This meant that seemingly ordinary environmental properties could be manipulated.
Examples:

* Weight zero made objects float.
* Changing the age of a tree altered its physical existence.
* Door numbers determined which rooms they connected to.
* Setting the clock to impossible values caused NPCs to disappear.
* An enormous number could be used to manipulate seemingly unrelated parts of the world.

The player gradually discovered the rules and found increasingly unconventional solutions.
It also contained a large collection of secrets, optional discoveries, hidden mechanics, and clever wordplay.

What worked particularly well
Both games had several qualities I particularly enjoyed:

1. A consistent underlying system with rules that could be discovered.
2. Mechanics that interacted with each other in unexpected ways.
3. Puzzles that encouraged experimentation rather than guessing arbitrary solutions.
4. New mechanics introduced in concentrated bursts.
5. Distinct environments with their own gameplay identities.
6. Secrets that rewarded curiosity.
7. An overarching idea that connected the world and its mechanics.
8. Meta gameplay that changed how I understood the experience.
9. A satisfying progression from a seemingly ordinary beginning to something much more unusual.

I particularly enjoyed discovering that something I had previously assumed was just a normal game mechanic actually had much deeper implications.
Use these games as examples of good design, not templates to reproduce.
Important: Do not repeat their central concepts.
Do not make another game centered on:

* Camera manipulation or screen visibility.
* Objects existing only when observed.
* Screen edges, screen wrapping, or split-screen physics.
* Physical number manipulation or exchanging digits with statistics.
* A world governed by numbers, counting, or arithmetic.
* A lighthouse that needs to be illuminated.
* Collecting glasses or lenses to restore a light.
* A post office and parcel delivery as the main adventure structure.
* The same clock and night-based secrets.

Both previous games ended with lighting a lighthouse. Absolutely do not repeat that ending.
I want something substantially more ambitious and surprising.

## 2. THE SIZE OF THIS PROJECT

This is the most important difference from previous assignments.
I do not want another short game.
I want a full-length, interconnected adventure.

Target playtime
Aim for:

* 10–14 hours for the main adventure.
* 15–20 hours for someone who explores extensively.
* 18–25 hours for someone who pursues most optional content and secrets.

These are design targets, not permission to pad the game with repetitive activities.
The hours should come from genuinely interesting exploration, dungeons, puzzles, discoveries, and progression.
Do not artificially increase playtime by making movement slow, adding excessive backtracking, or requiring repetitive grinding.

World size
Create a substantial interconnected overworld containing:

* A major central hub.
* At least 7 distinct overworld regions.
* At least 8 substantial main dungeons.
* At least 2 optional dungeons or major optional areas.
* Numerous smaller caves, hidden rooms, challenge areas, and secret locations.
* Multiple interconnected routes, shortcuts, and previously inaccessible locations.
* Meaningful areas that become accessible as the player gains new capabilities.

The regions should feel like parts of one coherent world.
They should not simply be separate levels selected from a menu.
Each should have its own identity, geography, environmental interactions, discoveries, characters, and relationship to the larger world.
The map should be interesting to explore independently of the main quest.

## 3. OVERALL GAME STRUCTURE

I want a Zelda-like adventure structure.
I am referring to the exploration and progression structure, not asking you to clone Zelda.
The game should have:

* An explorable overworld.
* A central hub that evolves throughout the adventure.
* Multiple regions with distinct identities.
* Dungeons containing puzzles, challenges, hidden rooms, and major encounters.
* New abilities, tools, or knowledge that expand where the player can go.
* Earlier locations that become accessible in new ways.
* Optional areas and secrets.
* A major overarching objective.
* A satisfying final adventure and conclusion.

The world should gradually open up.
I want to occasionally discover that somewhere I visited many hours ago now contains something I couldn't previously access.
I want to recognize landmarks, remember unusual locations, and become familiar with the geography.
Create meaningful interconnections between regions.
The world should have loops, shortcuts, surprising passages, hidden connections, and opportunities for backtracking with new capabilities.
Avoid making the map a collection of disconnected corridors.

The central hub
The hub is particularly important.
Make it a real explorable location, not just a menu or a place where NPCs stand around.
It should have:

* Multiple buildings and distinctive locations.
* Characters with individual personalities and purposes.
* Optional activities.
* Hidden passages and secrets.
* Locked areas that gradually become accessible.
* Characters whose circumstances change as the adventure progresses.
* New activities and interactions that appear over time.
* Connections to the larger story and hidden meta narrative.

The hub should visibly evolve.
The player should have reasons to return beyond simply saving or buying supplies.
It should also contain some of the game's best secrets.

## 4. DISTINCT DUNGEONS AND MECHANIC INTRODUCTION

This is one of the most important design requirements.
I particularly enjoy how you introduced mechanics in bursts in Seldom and Carry the One.
I want you to take that much further.
Every major dungeon should feel like a distinct gameplay experience.
Do not create eight dungeons that all use the same puzzle mechanics with different visual themes.
Each dungeon should introduce a substantial new gameplay concept, unusual interaction, physical rule, or puzzle grammar.
For example, one dungeon might be built around an unusual physical interaction, while another might introduce an entirely different way of manipulating objects, navigating space, interacting with creatures, or changing environmental rules.
I am intentionally not specifying the actual mechanics.
You must invent them.

Dungeon progression
Each major dungeon should follow a deliberate learning and discovery structure:

1. Introduce its central mechanic through an interesting encounter.
2. Let the player experiment with it.
3. Introduce increasingly complicated applications.
4. Combine it with existing mechanics.
5. Hide optional discoveries that reward deeper understanding.
6. Culminate in a major puzzle, encounter, or boss that tests the mechanic.

The player should feel that they have learned something new by the time they finish a dungeon.

Dungeon identity
Each dungeon needs its own:

* Visual identity.
* Environmental design.
* Puzzle vocabulary.
* Gameplay mechanic or major mechanic variation.
* Hazards and challenges.
* Secrets.
* Interesting encounters.
* Culminating challenge.

Some mechanics should remain useful throughout the rest of the game.
Others can be specific to particular areas.
Importantly, later dungeons should combine mechanics from earlier dungeons in unexpected ways.
I want to experience moments where I realize that two things I learned hours apart can interact.

Boss encounters
Bosses should interact with the mechanics of their respective dungeons.
Avoid generic enemies with large health bars.
Boss encounters should reward understanding the environment and the systems introduced in the dungeon.
Some should have multiple possible solutions.
Do not make bosses artificially difficult just to extend playtime.

## 5. THE CENTRAL GAMEPLAY CONCEPT

Despite having multiple different dungeon mechanics, the game needs an underlying identity.
Invent one genuinely interesting central gameplay concept.
The player should be able to experiment with the environment and discover unexpected properties and interactions.
I enjoy games where objects and environmental elements have meaningful underlying behaviors rather than being decorative props with predetermined interactions.
Develop a consistent simulation or interaction framework where practical.
For example, objects might have properties that affect their physical behavior, relationships, movement, or interactions with other systems.
However, do not simply give everything arbitrary statistics.
The interactions should make intuitive sense once the player understands the underlying rules.
Allow creative applications that you did not explicitly design individual puzzles around.
Do not hardcode every possible solution.
The player should occasionally solve a challenge in an unexpected way because they understood the mechanics.
The central mechanic should be original and should not simply reproduce Seldom's camera system or Carry the One's numerical manipulation system.

## 6. THE GAME MUST EVOLVE

One of my favorite ideas is a game that gradually changes genre or changes the player's understanding of what they are doing.
I want this to happen over the course of the main adventure.
The beginning should establish an understandable experience.
As the player progresses, the game should gradually introduce evidence that something much more unusual is happening.
At some point, the actual nature of the gameplay should change substantially.
This should be a meaningful transformation, not a random minigame inserted halfway through.
It should affect the player's understanding of:

* The world.
* The mechanics.
* The objective.
* The player's role.
* The meaning of previous events.

The transformation should be foreshadowed, but not obvious.
Do not reveal this in early dialogue or tutorials.
I want the realization to come through playing the game.

## 7. A LARGE, CONNECTED META NARRATIVE

This is another major requirement.
I want a substantial overarching meta concept that connects the entire adventure.
Do not treat meta gameplay as a single joke or surprise in the final cutscene.
Build it throughout the entire experience.
The hidden concept should have connections to:

* The overworld.
* The hub.
* The dungeons.
* NPCs.
* Environmental details.
* The underlying gameplay systems.
* Optional secrets.
* The interface and presentation.
* The player's progression.
* The game's ending.

Include recurring details that initially appear insignificant but acquire new meaning later.
Some secrets should reveal additional information about the central mystery.
Other secrets should reveal entirely different applications of the mechanics.
The late-game realization should change how the player understands the previous several hours.

The major reveal
I want at least one substantial reveal that genuinely challenges the player's expectations.
It should not simply be:

* Everything was a dream.
* The player was secretly a character's imagination.
* The world was a computer simulation.
* A random character was secretly the villain.
* A surprise ending unrelated to the preceding gameplay.

Invent something more interesting.
The reveal must be supported by the game's mechanics and earlier details.
Ideally, discovering the truth should change what the player can do, how the player interacts with the world, or what the final objective actually means.
I want the realization to feel surprising but retrospectively logical.
Do not make the previous adventure feel meaningless.
I want to think back on earlier experiences and realize that there were clues everywhere.
The ending should provide a satisfying payoff for the entire journey.

## 8. SECRETS AND OPTIONAL CONTENT

I particularly enjoy secrets that are discovered through experimentation.
Do not rely entirely on conventional hidden chests or collectible items.
Create several different types of optional discoveries.
Include:

* Small environmental secrets.
* Hidden rooms.
* Optional puzzle challenges.
* Unusual interactions.
* Multi-stage secrets spanning multiple regions.
* NPC-related discoveries.
* Hidden applications of existing mechanics.
* Optional dungeon areas.
* At least one substantial secret quest that requires knowledge gathered across the game.

Aim for at least 25–35 meaningful optional discoveries across the complete adventure.
Not all discoveries need to be difficult.
Some can be funny, strange, surprising, rewarding, or simply interesting.
Include a small number of secrets that are so unusual that I would be unlikely to discover them without experimenting.
Some should reward mechanical knowledge rather than exploration alone.
Avoid putting every optional discovery on the map.

Secret progression
I want at least one major optional secret chain that takes considerable effort to uncover.
It should connect multiple otherwise unrelated parts of the game.
The player should gradually realize that the clues are related.
This secret should have a meaningful payoff rather than just awarding a collectible.

## 9. ART DIRECTION AND PRESENTATION

I enjoy the visual personality of classic Nintendo and Dragon Quest games.
I like colorful, readable, expressive environments and characters.
I do not require a particular graphics style.
Choose whatever visual approach best supports the game.
However, the game must have a cohesive identity.
Avoid:

* Generic placeholder graphics.
* Inconsistent art styles.
* Excessive reliance on primitive geometric shapes.
* Repetitive environments.
* Generic fantasy assets.
* An anime aesthetic by default.

I want the world to feel deliberately designed.
Prioritize:

* Distinctive environments.
* Expressive characters.
* Clear visual communication.
* Interesting environmental animations.
* Good movement and interaction feedback.
* Appropriate sound effects.
* Music that contributes to the atmosphere.
* Memorable dungeon presentation.

The hub and regions should have recognizable visual identities.
Each dungeon should feel visually different from the others.
Do not sacrifice visual quality simply to add more content.
